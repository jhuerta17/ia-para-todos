# Responsible AI

> **DRAFT:** Proposed teaching and operating standards. Untested — refine after pilot workshops. Bilingual key rules included since facilitators teach these verbatim.

These standards bind both **what we teach** (every workshop) and **how we operate** (tools we use, examples we show).

## 1. Hallucinations are the default assumption

AI tools generate fluent text that may be partly or wholly false — names, dates, laws, remedies, quotes. Teach participants to expect errors, not be surprised by them.

- Demonstrate a verifiable error live in Workshop 1; practice spotting suspicious precision (exact dates, named prizes, confident niche facts).
- Teach the challenge prompt: *"¿De dónde sacaste ese dato? ¿Qué tan seguro estás?"*
- **Key rule / Regla clave:** *"Si es importante, lo verifico en otro lado."*

## 2. Verification of important information

- **Always verify independently:** health, legal, immigration, financial, employment, and school decisions — via professionals, official sources, or trusted organizations, never via a second AI answer alone.
- Teach the ladder: AI draft → compare with an authoritative source → ask a qualified human for high-stakes matters.
- Family version (Workshop 4): *"Pausa, duda y verifica"* before believing or sharing.

## 3. Never enter sensitive data

Prohibited in all exercises and demos — real personal identity, addresses, phone numbers, documents; medical, financial, legal, or immigration details (own or others'); children's names, schools, or photos; client or employer data; passwords or account access. Use fictional examples only. If a participant starts typing real data, intervene kindly and immediately.

## 4. Privacy and data retention

- Assume anything typed into a free AI tool may be stored, reviewed, or used for training. Teach participants to act accordingly: general or fictional inputs only.
- Do not create accounts for participants; do not ask for their credentials; do not photograph their screens.
- The project itself retains no participant inputs — feedback is anonymous and aggregate (see [privacy-guidelines.md](privacy-guidelines.md)).

## 5. Bias and discrimination

- AI systems reflect biases in their training data: they can stereotype by language, accent, name, neighborhood, or background, and give worse answers in Spanish than in English for some topics.
- Teach participants to notice and challenge biased outputs (*"esa respuesta me parece injusta, dame otra perspectiva"*) and never to treat AI judgments about people (hiring, lending, policing, schooling) as neutral or authoritative.

## 6. AI-generated scams

Cover the common patterns: fake prize/fee demands, impersonated package notices, urgent money requests, "verify your account" links, and voice/chat impersonation of relatives or officials. Teach the standing rules: no codes or money via messages; verify through a separate channel; legitimate institutions don't demand secrecy or urgency.

## 7. Deepfakes and impersonation

- Explain that realistic fake photos, audio, and video are now easy to make; show what "too perfect, no source, emotional urgency" looks like (Workshop 4 tarjetas).
- Family protocol: agree on a verification word or callback routine for "emergency" calls; teach youth (future programs) never to share images that could be misused.
- The project never creates deepfakes, even as demos — describe and show detection, don't produce fakes.

## 8. Appropriate use in education

- AI as tutor (explains, quizzes, adapts) — yes. AI as ghostwriter (does the work, student learns nothing) — no: it bypasses learning, may contain errors, and can bring school sanctions.
- Teach students' families to ask schools about their AI policies and to follow them.
- Our own materials model this: we show both the good prompt and why the copy-paste prompt fails.

## 9. Hard limits: health, legal, immigration, financial decisions

- AI output must never be presented as professional advice. Our curricula state plainly: consult licensed professionals and qualified community organizations.
- Facilitators do not interpret diagnoses, contracts, cases, or benefits with AI in front of participants; they explain the limit and suggest where to seek human help.
- No workshop exercise touches a real high-stakes scenario — all examples are fictional and low-stakes.

## Operational compliance for the team

- Use only reputable free tools; no tool that requires excessive permissions or payment for basic use; no vendor endorsements in materials.
- Review curricula quarterly for drift (tool behavior changes; new scam patterns emerge).
- Log any responsible-AI incident (misleading demo, data exposure, scam question raised) privately and fix the process.
